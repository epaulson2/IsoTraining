# Squat / Knee-Dominant Family

## Status

**Validated technical draft — historical geometry is strong for the wall squat and single-leg squat, while some extension variants remain provisional.**

## Family role

This family develops squat-position control, quadriceps and hip loading, lower-body stabilization, and the ability to maintain prescribed joint geometry while actively pulling deeper into position.

Unlike a generic wall sit or squat hold, the historical Schroeder / EVO intent is not simply to remain motionless. The athlete begins at the deepest acceptable position and continues attempting to move farther into that position without abandoning the prescribed geometry.

## Primary versions

### A. Iso-Extreme Wall Squat
- **Type:** Integrated / supported
- **Provenance:** Schroeder / EVO Original
- **Role:** Core bilateral knee-dominant position

### B. Iso-Extreme Single-Leg Squat
- **Type:** Integrated unilateral
- **Provenance:** Schroeder / EVO Original / strong historical description
- **Role:** Advanced unilateral knee-dominant position

### C. Supported Deep Squat Iso
- **Type:** Integrated regression / derivative
- **Provenance:** Schroeder-Derived
- **Role:** Learn Mode and unsupported squat-position acquisition

### D. Strap-Assisted Knee-Dominant Iso
- **Type:** Targeted / hybrid
- **Provenance:** Evidence-Based Extension
- **Role:** Increase quadriceps / knee-extensor intent while reducing balance as the limiting factor

# A. Iso-Extreme Wall Squat

## Historical confidence

High. Schroeder directly described the position as an Iso-Extreme example: begin at the deepest wall-squat position the athlete can attain, then continue pulling toward the floor while maintaining an upright torso, lower legs perpendicular to the floor, feet flat, and arms at the sides.

## Purpose

Teach bilateral squat geometry, muscular control at a deep knee-flexion angle, and sustained active intent without using forward trunk lean or ankle movement to escape the position.

## Setup geometry

- Back against a stable wall.
- Torso upright rather than folded forward.
- Feet placed far enough forward that the **lower legs can remain approximately perpendicular to the floor** at the working depth.
- Feet remain flat in the primary Schroeder-described version.
- Knees track in the general direction of the feet rather than collapsing inward.
- Arms remain relaxed at the sides in the historical reference version; do not use the hands on the thighs for assistance.

## Working depth

The athlete descends to the deepest position that still allows:

- upright trunk;
- lower legs approximately vertical;
- feet flat;
- controlled knee tracking;
- active muscular effort rather than passive joint loading.

Parallel is not mandatory if the athlete cannot maintain the required geometry there. Schroeder's own explanation explicitly uses “whatever extreme position you can get into.”

## Pressure strategy

For the primary version, preserve full-foot contact. Later coaching reports describe heel- and forefoot-biased variants, but these should be treated as separate variations rather than silently substituted for the baseline position.

## Active intent

The defining cue is:

**Actively pull yourself farther toward the floor while maintaining the wall-squat geometry.**

Do not merely sit against the wall.

A useful secondary cue, supported by later EVO-derived coaching, is to create a backward-dragging action through the feet / hamstrings without allowing the feet to move. This should be introduced only after the trainee can maintain the primary geometry.

## What should remain stable

- torso remains upright;
- shins remain approximately vertical;
- feet remain flat;
- knees remain controlled over the feet;
- pelvis does not repeatedly slide or tilt to chase depth;
- head remains neutral.

## What you should feel

Primary muscular effort should be through the thighs and hips, with a sense of active whole-leg tension rather than sharp pressure in the knees.

The exact distribution between quadriceps, hamstrings and glutes can vary with intent and individual anatomy. The exercise should not feel like passive compression at the front of the knee.

## Learn Mode sequence

### Step 1 — Find foot distance
Stand against the wall and slide down slightly. Adjust the feet forward until the shins can remain nearly vertical.

### Step 2 — Establish full-foot pressure
Keep heel, base of big toe and base of little toe in contact.

### Step 3 — Set knee tracking
Match the knee path to the general direction of the toes.

### Step 4 — Keep the torso upright
Use the wall for support rather than folding forward.

### Step 5 — Find the deepest clean position
Descend only until the geometry can still be maintained.

### Step 6 — Compare passive versus active
Briefly feel the difference between simply resting on the wall and actively generating muscular tension.

### Step 7 — Add downward intent
Without changing the prescribed geometry, attempt to pull the body farther toward the floor.

### Step 8 — Add optional hamstring drag-back intent
At low effort, try to drag the feet backward against the floor without allowing them to move. If this disrupts foot contact or knee position, remove the cue and return to the primary Schroeder description.

### Step 9 — Competency hold
Hold 10–20 seconds while preserving upright torso, vertical shins, flat feet and active downward intent.

### Step 10 — Practice the exit
Reduce intent first, then stand deliberately rather than collapsing out of the position.

## Common compensations

- **Feet too close to wall / knees drift far forward:** move feet forward and rebuild vertical-shin geometry.
- **Heels lift:** reduce depth or adjust foot distance.
- **Torso folds forward:** reduce depth and restore upright position.
- **Knees collapse inward:** reduce depth and re-establish foot pressure / tracking.
- **Passive wall sit:** restore active downward intent.
- **Using hands on thighs:** remove hand assistance unless it is an intentional regression.

## Regressions

- shallower depth;
- shorter duration;
- reduced intent;
- supported standing squat position before full wall-squat depth.

## Advanced variations

- longer accumulated duration;
- heel-biased or forefoot-biased variations only when intentionally programmed;
- unilateral wall-squat variation;
- Extreme Slow wall squat;
- later pairing with dynamic / rebound work where appropriate.

## Workout Mode cues

**Upright torso • shins vertical / feet flat • actively pull lower**

# B. Iso-Extreme Single-Leg Squat

## Historical confidence

Moderate-to-high. Historical Schroeder descriptions are unusually specific, but not all surviving accounts are perfectly consistent. Treat the following as the best-supported geometry rather than a universal law for every later EVO variation.

## Purpose

Develop unilateral squat-position control, hip / knee stabilization and high local lower-body demand while maintaining a precise body position.

## Historical setup

One detailed historical description places the working foot on a box or bench, with the athlete supported primarily through the **front / ball of the working foot**, heel elevated, upper body erect, glute engaged, and the working thigh lowered to approximately parallel.

Other historical accounts describe a one-leg wall-squat variation with the non-working leg supported on a box. These appear to be related but distinct implementations.

Because the single-leg versions vary across surviving sources, the manual should present one baseline version at a time rather than merging them.

## Baseline version for this project

### Box-Supported Single-Leg Squat Iso

- Stand on one foot on a stable box / bench.
- Working foot faces forward or slightly outward according to comfortable knee tracking.
- Use forefoot-dominant pressure only if the trainee can maintain stable ankle and knee alignment.
- Keep upper body erect.
- Keep pelvis level rather than dropping the non-working side.
- Descend until the working thigh approaches parallel **without losing control**.
- Keep the non-working leg positioned so it does not provide hidden support.

## Active intent

Historical descriptions emphasize active glute contribution and maintaining the “perfect” squat position under time. A separate archived comment attributed to Schroeder emphasizes pulling the working leg into greater extension while preserving spinal extension.

Because those descriptions are not perfectly harmonized, our baseline instructional cue is:

**Maintain the exact single-leg squat geometry and actively create upward / extension intent through the working leg without allowing the body to rise.**

This preserves the high-intent isometric principle without inventing a more specific force vector than the evidence supports.

## What should remain stable

- pelvis stays level;
- knee tracks over the foot;
- torso remains erect;
- working foot pressure remains controlled;
- non-working leg does not assist;
- no uncontrolled twisting of the hip or trunk.

## Learn Mode sequence

### Step 1 — Learn the support setup
Use a rail / rack lightly with the hands so balance does not dominate the exercise.

### Step 2 — Establish single-leg foot pressure
Find a stable pressure strategy before descending.

### Step 3 — Keep pelvis level
Prevent the free-side hip from dropping or rotating open.

### Step 4 — Track the knee
Maintain knee alignment over the foot.

### Step 5 — Descend gradually
Stop before the pelvis, knee or foot loses control.

### Step 6 — Establish extension intent
At low effort, attempt to drive through the working leg without allowing the body to rise.

### Step 7 — Remove excess hand support
Use the hands only for balance once geometry is reliable.

### Step 8 — Competency hold
Hold 8–15 seconds with level pelvis, stable knee and repeatable pressure.

## Common compensations

- pelvis drops or rotates;
- knee collapses inward;
- trunk leans excessively to counterbalance;
- non-working leg touches support;
- foot rolls excessively inward / outward;
- athlete chases parallel depth after geometry is already lost.

## Regressions

- bilateral wall squat;
- assisted split squat / lunge;
- supported single-leg squat at shallower depth;
- full-foot pressure before forefoot-biased historical variation;
- shorter competency holds.

## Advanced variations

- reduce external balance support;
- increase accumulated duration;
- historical forefoot-dominant version if appropriate;
- unilateral wall-squat version;
- Extreme Slow single-leg squat;
- later dynamic integration.

## Workout Mode cues

**Level pelvis • stable knee / foot • drive through the working leg without rising**

# C. Supported Deep Squat Iso

## Provenance

**Schroeder-Derived.** Historical material clearly includes squat / wall-squat positions, but this supported free-space version is primarily a teaching bridge rather than a uniquely documented canonical exercise.

## Purpose

Teach unsupported squat depth, foot pressure and whole-body stabilization before the athlete is asked to perform a harder free-space extreme hold.

## Setup

- Hold a rack, post or suspension support lightly.
- Use a stance that permits comfortable hip and knee flexion.
- Keep full-foot contact.
- Descend only as deep as the pelvis, knees and feet remain controlled.
- Use the hands for balance, not to unload most of the bodyweight.

## Active intent

Once the position is stable, attempt to **pull deeper into the squat while preserving active foot pressure and trunk control**.

## Learn Mode role

This variation is especially useful when the trainee cannot yet distinguish:

- balance failure from muscular failure;
- passive bottom-range hanging from active control;
- true squat depth from compensatory lumbar / pelvic movement.

## Workout Mode cues

**Full foot • controlled knees / pelvis • pull deeper without hanging**

# D. Strap-Assisted Knee-Dominant Iso

## Provenance

**Evidence-Based Extension.** This is not presented as a Schroeder original.

## Purpose

Use a non-elastic strap to create a precise immovable knee-extension / squat resistance while reducing balance as the limiting factor.

## Candidate implementations

1. **Strap-supported squat hold:** strap used as external balance support while the athlete actively maintains squat position.
2. **Overcoming knee-extension iso:** strap arranged so the athlete attempts to extend the knees against an immovable endpoint at a chosen joint angle.
3. **Hybrid squat + strap pull:** athlete maintains squat geometry while pulling against the strap to increase whole-body tension.

Only the first two should be considered for early programming. The hybrid version requires further mechanical validation.

## Learn Mode principles

- Set strap length before high-intent effort.
- Choose a joint angle that can be maintained without sharp knee pressure.
- Establish foot and knee alignment before adding force.
- Build tension gradually.
- Stop if strap position changes the intended joint geometry.

## Why this complements the integrated squat

The integrated wall / free-space squat challenges positional control and stabilization. The strap version can concentrate force at a chosen knee angle and reduce balance demands. They are complementary rather than interchangeable.

# Programming role across levels

## Beginner

Primary:
- Wall Squat
- Supported Deep Squat Iso

Optional:
- low-intent strap-assisted knee-dominant iso

Avoid making the single-leg squat a required beginner exercise.

## Intermediate

Primary:
- Wall Squat
- Single-Leg Squat regression / assisted version
- Strap Knee-Dominant Iso

## Advanced

Primary:
- Single-Leg Squat
- unilateral wall-squat variants
- longer / higher-intent wall-squat exposures
- Extreme Slow squat variations
- strategic pairing with dynamic force-absorption / rebound work
