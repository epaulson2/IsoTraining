# IsoTraining Programming Principles

## Status

**Phase 2 source of truth — locked programming framework.**

This document defines how IsoTraining sessions are programmed. Week-by-week plans should follow these rules unless a later revision explicitly changes the framework.

## 1. Core programming model

IsoTraining uses two distinct isometric modes rather than treating every hold the same.

### A. Position / Extreme-Iso work
Purpose: positional control, long-position tolerance, integrated stabilization, force distribution, and sustained intent.

Typical characteristics:
- submaximal to moderately high effort;
- longer holds or accumulated time;
- position quality is the limiting factor;
- often bodyweight or integrated positions;
- the athlete continues creating directional intent rather than passively holding.

### B. High-intent targeted isometrics
Purpose: high force production at a selected joint angle, local tissue loading, and targeted strength work.

Typical characteristics:
- short efforts;
- high voluntary force intent;
- usually strap- or support-assisted;
- precise joint angle;
- longer rest between efforts.

**Rule:** long duration and high intent are separate variables. A 3-minute hold is not assumed to be maximal effort, and a maximal-intent effort is not expected to last for minutes.

## 2. Effort scale

Use a simple 1–10 effort scale.

- 3–4/10: learning / rehearsal
- 5–6/10: foundational position work
- 7–8/10: hard training effort with technical reserve
- 9/10: advanced high-intent work
- 10/10: true maximal attempt; rarely required and not used by beginners

### Default effort by mode

**Position / Extreme-Iso:** mostly 5–8/10.

**Targeted high-intent iso:** mostly 7–9/10 after the movement is learned.

Beginners should not use 9–10/10 efforts.

## 3. Hold-duration framework

### Learn Mode
- 6–15 seconds per rehearsal
- 3–5/10 effort
- stop well before fatigue
- purpose is geometry + intent, not conditioning

### Beginner Position Work
- 10–30 seconds per bout
- usually 2–4 bouts
- typical accumulated time: 30–90 seconds per exercise

### Intermediate Position Work
- 20–45 seconds per bout
- usually 2–4 bouts
- typical accumulated time: 60–150 seconds per exercise

### Advanced Position Work
- 30–60+ seconds per bout where technically appropriate
- accumulated time may progress toward 2–5 minutes on selected Schroeder-style positions
- the full 3–5 minute concept is an advanced exposure, not a universal target

### High-Intent Targeted Work
- 5–10 seconds per effort
- usually 3–5 efforts
- stop if force quality drops sharply or geometry changes

## 4. Rest periods

### Between Position-Work bouts
- 30–75 seconds for smaller / easier positions
- 60–120 seconds for demanding integrated positions

### Between High-Intent efforts
- 60–120 seconds for smaller muscle groups
- 90–180 seconds for large compound or very high-intent efforts

### Between exercises
- usually 1–3 minutes as needed to restore technique and intent

Rest is not minimized for conditioning purposes. The priority is quality.

## 5. Weekly frequency

### Beginner
- 3 sessions per week
- each major movement family typically trained 2–3 times per week through a small rotating selection
- avoid repeating a hard high-intent version for the same tissue on consecutive days

### Intermediate
- 3–4 sessions per week
- each major movement family typically receives 2 meaningful exposures per week
- integrated and targeted variants may be split across sessions

### Advanced
- typically 4 sessions per week
- selected tissues / movement families may receive 2–3 exposures when intensity and volume are distributed appropriately
- high-intent, long-duration and reactive methods should not all peak simultaneously

## 6. Integrated vs targeted balance

The program intentionally preserves both.

### Integrated positions
Examples: Extreme Lunge, Wall Squat, Extreme Push-Up, Body-Supported Row, Scapular Hang, Lateral Lunge.

Primary role:
- whole-body organization;
- stabilization;
- force distribution;
- posture under fatigue;
- coordination between prime movers and stabilizers.

### Targeted positions
Examples: Strap Chest Press, Strap Overhead Triceps, Strap External Rotation, Strap Curl.

Primary role:
- precise joint-angle loading;
- local high-intent force;
- easier manipulation of muscle length and leverage;
- reduced balance limitation.

### Pairing rule

Do not automatically pair both versions of the same family in every session.

Use one of three strategies:

1. **Alternate** — integrated on one day, targeted on another.
2. **Complement** — integrated position first, targeted high-intent work second.
3. **Specialize** — temporarily emphasize one version when a specific adaptation is desired.

For beginners, default toward integrated work plus a small amount of targeted work.

## 7. Exercise-tier rules

### Tier A — Programming Core
These exercises form the backbone of beginner and intermediate plans.

### Tier B — Secondary / Developmental
Used after competency in the Tier A pattern or when a specific need justifies them.

### Tier C — Optional / Accessory
Not required for program completeness. Use sparingly.

**Rule:** programming should not become more advanced simply by adding more exercises. Difficulty should progress mainly through better position, more duration, more intent, greater leverage/load, and later method integration.

## 8. Progression hierarchy

Progress only one major variable at a time whenever practical.

### Position-work progression
1. Learn the geometry.
2. Hold the position without compensation.
3. Improve active internal force intent.
4. Increase bout duration.
5. Increase accumulated time.
6. Increase effort.
7. Increase leverage or external load.
8. Add Extreme Slow or advanced dynamic pairing.

### Targeted-isometric progression
1. Learn joint angle and strap setup.
2. Produce repeatable moderate force.
3. Increase effort.
4. Add sets / total high-quality efforts.
5. Manipulate joint angle when useful.
6. Add load / leverage only if setup remains stable.

## 9. Progression gates

A trainee may progress only when all are true:

- the setup can be reproduced without coaching;
- intended pressure / tension is felt in the target region;
- the correct force direction is maintained;
- no major compensation appears near the end of the set;
- the exit remains controlled;
- no sharp pain, pinching, numbness or tingling occurs;
- the current prescription has been completed cleanly in at least two sessions.

If only the clock improved but geometry deteriorated, no progression occurred.

## 10. Stop criteria

End a set when any of the following occurs:

- loss of the defining joint geometry;
- transition from active muscular tension to passive hanging;
- repeated knee / shoulder / ankle collapse;
- inability to maintain the intended force direction;
- breath-holding becomes uncontrolled despite cueing;
- sharp pain, pinching, numbness, tingling, or sudden joint discomfort;
- force falls so much that the set becomes survival rather than purposeful training.

Technical failure ends the set before absolute muscular failure.

## 11. Breathing

Normal breathing is the default during longer position work.

During high-intent short efforts, brief bracing is acceptable, but the trainee should avoid prolonged straining or repeated uncontrolled breath-holding.

## 12. Session-volume guardrails

### Beginner
- 4–6 exercises per session
- 2–4 work bouts per exercise
- mostly Tier A
- usually no more than 1–2 targeted high-intent exercises in the same session

### Intermediate
- 5–7 exercises per session
- mix of Tier A and selected Tier B
- usually 1–3 targeted high-intent exercises per session

### Advanced
- volume depends on split and method emphasis
- avoid maximal duration + maximal intent + reactive work for the same tissue in one session unless deliberately programmed and well tolerated

## 13. Deload / consolidation logic

IsoTraining uses a default **3 build weeks + 1 consolidation week** rhythm when programs extend beyond four weeks.

During consolidation week:
- reduce total accumulated time by about 30–40%;
- reduce high-intent sets by about one third;
- keep technique and frequency largely intact;
- do not test maximal hold times.

This is a project programming convention, not a claim that every trainee physiologically requires a fixed 4-week deload cycle.

## 14. Beginner-to-intermediate graduation

A beginner is ready for intermediate programming when they can:

- independently set up the major Tier A positions;
- perform 20–30 second clean integrated holds on the main patterns;
- accumulate roughly 60–90 seconds of quality work per main exercise without technical collapse;
- perform short strap isometrics at roughly 7/10 effort without posture changes;
- distinguish muscular effort from passive stretch or joint pressure;
- recover normally between three weekly sessions.

## 15. Intermediate-to-advanced graduation

Intermediate trainees may enter advanced programming when they can:

- maintain stable geometry under longer accumulated work;
- produce repeatable 8–9/10 high-intent strap efforts;
- tolerate higher-leverage integrated positions without compensation;
- use Tier B exercises without losing the Tier A fundamentals;
- recover from four weekly sessions;
- demonstrate readiness before Extreme Slow, altitude-drop, rebound or reactive pairings are introduced.

## 16. Advanced-method sequencing

Advanced methods are layered in this order:

1. longer accumulated Extreme-Iso work;
2. increased leverage / loading;
3. Extreme Slow;
4. controlled force-absorption work;
5. rebound / reactive pairing;
6. sport- or task-specific expression where relevant.

The trainee should not be pushed into reactive work merely because they have completed a calendar phase.

## 17. Evidence / rationale summary

The programming framework is informed by modern isometric literature showing that adaptation depends on muscle length, contraction intensity and intent; longer-muscle-length training can improve hypertrophy and transfer in some contexts, while higher-intensity contractions are more relevant to tendon adaptation. The evidence does not support treating every isometric prescription as interchangeable.

The project therefore preserves Schroeder-style long-duration positional work while separating it from short high-intent isometrics and from later reactive methods.

## 18. Safety scope

This manual is for general training, not diagnosis or rehabilitation of injury. Painful or symptomatic joints, known cardiovascular limitations, recent surgery, or active tendon injury may require individualized clinical guidance rather than generic progression rules.
